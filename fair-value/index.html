Pricing Evaluation Accounts as Options

Advantage Play – Independent quantitative project · Nov 2025 – Apr 2026

  • HTML / CSS / JavaScript
  • Monte Carlo Simulation
  • Contract & Rulebook Analysis

Question & Research

Question:

Are evaluation companies' accounts priced below the fair value of the leverage being sold?

Research:

Evaluation companies market themselves to retail traders as "prop firms," which is the term behind the search trend below. They sell a paid test: you pay a fee for a simulated account with a set drawdown amount. If you hit the profit target without breaching the trailing drawdown, you move to a "funded" account, where you can request payouts once you pass a set profit threshold. If you breach the drawdown, the account is gone and you've lost only the fee.

That's the shape of an option: a fixed cost, a capped downside, and an upside you can collect.

Profit Structure of Evaluation Companies:

These companies earn most of their revenue from fees, not from trading. Customers pay for evaluations, and most fail. That fee revenue funds payouts to the small share who reach a funded account and qualify.

Funded accounts are typically simulated: orders don't reach the market, so payouts come from the company's own revenue, not from trading profits. Topstep's own 2025 statistics show that only 0.71% of its funded traders were moved to a live account.

The business model is a casino's: fees are priced so that total fee revenue exceeds total payouts, and the house wins on average. The customers these companies want are the ones who fail. A customer who is paid out consistently is a cost, and a company can limit that cost by changing its rules or ending the relationship.

Hypothesis & Model

Hypothesis:

If the expected fees spent to get an account to its payout threshold are less than the expected payout value it produces, then the account structure has positive expected value.

How an Account Maps to an Option:

Account featureOption equivalent
Account balanceS – Spot
Evaluation and activation feesP – Premium
Trailing drawdownB – Knock-out barrier
Profit needed to qualify for a payoutK – Strike
Payout amountC – Capped payoff
What the account is actually worthV – Fair value

*The most you can lose on an account is its fees. The upside is paid in fixed chunks once the account qualifies.

Model:

I built a Monte Carlo simulator that models a funded account's lifecycle, complete with Apex Trader Funding's specific parameters at the time.

  • Simulates trades with zero expected value before commissions
  • Applies the company's actual rules: intraday trailing drawdown, trailing cap, commissions, payout thresholds, winning-day rules

Experiment

Run 1 · Accounts Bought Jan 30 – Feb 27, 2026

  • 70 evaluations at $19.70 and 29 funded accounts at $75, for $3,554.00 in fees

Run 2 · Accounts Bought Apr 1 – Apr 11, 2026

  • 58 evaluations at $24.90 and 18 funded accounts at $99, for $3,226.20 in fees, plus 2 accounts carried over from Run 1

*The runs overlap because it takes weeks to scale in and out of accounts. They're split by the rule and price change, not by a clean end date.

*Run 2's trades weren't recorded. I placed them from my phone with a fixed take profit and stop loss, choosing direction on a literal coin flip, while working full-time night shifts. It didn't need to be perfect; the window was closing and I needed to act on it.

Analysis

Results:

The trading itself had no edge, as designed. The results came from the accounts that survived long enough to pay out, while each account that failed cost only its fee.

Actual payouts averaged 87% of the model's predictions. In both runs, about 70% of accounts never paid out, against roughly 80% expected.

I attribute the variation to three main factors:

  • Market-order entries, especially at night. The spread on NQ overnight is much wider than during the day, so a market entry had slightly worse odds than the model assumed
  • Stop losses are market orders too. They trigger on Rithmic's servers, and some filled with large slippage
  • Rithmic and personal internet outages. At the height of evaluation-company popularity, Rithmic went down frequently. Several trades lost far more than should have been permitted, like the −$3,800 outlier in the Experiment charts

Rule Changes between Runs:

Between the two runs, the company raised its prices and cut its payouts. That's consistent with the company noticing it was priced under fair value, and with the window of opportunity closing.

How It Ended:

Midway through Run 2, many of my accounts were still in the money when the company "upgraded" me to a live-funded account. That's a polite way of declining further business. I had worked within its stated rules the whole time, which is why I was promoted rather than banned. I declined the live account, took my payouts, and the project ended. A final $3,000 payment arrived about three months later.

Conclusion

The hypothesis held. Across 128 evaluations, trading with no market edge still produced $32.4k in payouts on $6.8k of fees, because the accounts were priced below the value of the payout options they carried.

I watched the edge shrink in real time. Over a matter of weeks, the company's rules changed and brought its accounts closer to fair value. This company and many others like it have since strengthened their contracts and pricing to prevent what this project did.

What I Took from It:

  • There will always be uncontrollable factors in modeling; here it was counterparty risk, with my accounts closed and "promoted to live"
  • I had to decide to accept small bugs and missing validation in order to pursue the project in a timely manner
  • Pricing inefficiencies close, so the first version that's right enough beats a perfect one that's late

What I Could Have Done Better:

  • Automated limit-order entries from a more stable connection
  • Logged every trade with more detailed accounting
  • Built expected expenses into the model

Potential Next Steps:

  • Implement the option-like structure of evaluation accounts into an overall portfolio
  • Automate screening of popular evaluation companies' rules to identify mispricings faster
  • Identify other retail products that may follow a similar structure

*Figures come from my account records, payout history, and bank deposits, available upon request.
